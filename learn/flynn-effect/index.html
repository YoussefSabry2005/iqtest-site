The Flynn effect: why IQ scores rose for decades

What the Flynn effect is, the leading explanations for rising test scores, and why tests have to be re-normed regularly.

What it is

The political scientist James Flynn documented that average performance on IQ tests rose substantially across the 20th century in many countries, by roughly three points per decade on average. Because tests are re-normed to keep the average at 100, the rise was hidden unless you compared raw scores across generations.

Possible explanations

Researchers have proposed several contributing causes, including better nutrition and health, more and longer schooling, smaller families, and everyday environments that increasingly reward abstract thinking. Most researchers think several factors acted together rather than one single cause.

Recent changes

In some countries, notably in Scandinavia, studies of military conscripts found that the rise slowed or even reversed from the 1990s onward. Explanations for this are still debated.

Why it matters

The Flynn effect is a reminder that IQ scores are always relative to a particular reference group at a particular time. It is one reason professional tests are re-normed regularly, and why norms from one site or era shouldn't be applied to another.

What it does not mean

Rising scores do not necessarily mean each generation is "smarter" in every sense. The gains were largest on abstract reasoning tasks such as matrices, and smaller on vocabulary and general knowledge. Many researchers interpret the effect as a change in the kinds of thinking people practise, rather than a simple rise in a single underlying ability.
