Fluid vs crystallized intelligence: two kinds of thinking

The difference between fluid reasoning and crystallized knowledge, how each changes with age, and which kind reasoning puzzles measure.

Two broad abilities

The psychologist Raymond Cattell, later working with John Horn, distinguished two broad kinds of intelligence. Fluid intelligence is the ability to reason through new problems you have not seen before: spotting patterns, working out rules and thinking logically. Crystallized intelligence is accumulated knowledge and skill: vocabulary, facts and learned methods.

How they change with age

Research consistently finds that fluid reasoning tends to peak in early adulthood and then declines gradually, while crystallized knowledge tends to stay stable or keep growing well into later adulthood. Both trends are averages; individuals vary a lot.

Which one puzzles measure

Matrix puzzles, number series and mental rotation are classic measures of fluid reasoning, because they rely on finding a new rule rather than recalling a fact. That is why this site uses nonsense words in its logic questions: they remove the advantage of prior knowledge, so you are reasoning rather than remembering.

Why the distinction is useful

A low score on unfamiliar puzzles says nothing about your knowledge or expertise, and vice versa. Seeing both kinds of ability helps explain why people can be strong in very different ways.

Can you train them?

Crystallized knowledge clearly grows with learning, reading and experience. For fluid reasoning, practice reliably improves performance on the specific kinds of puzzles you practise, but evidence that it raises general reasoning ability across the board is much weaker. Either way, regular mental challenge, learning new skills and staying physically healthy are good for thinking at every age.
