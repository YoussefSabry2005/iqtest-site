What is an IQ test? A plain-English guide

Where IQ tests came from, what they measure, how modern scores are calculated, and how a free online test differs from a professional assessment.

A short history

The first practical intelligence test was created by Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon in France in 1905 to identify schoolchildren who needed extra support. The German psychologist William Stern later proposed the "intelligence quotient": mental age divided by chronological age, multiplied by 100. Lewis Terman adapted Binet's work in the United States as the Stanford-Binet test, and David Wechsler's scales, first published in the 1930s and 1950s, introduced the approach almost all modern tests use today.

How modern IQ scores work

Today's tests no longer use the mental-age ratio. Instead they use a "deviation IQ": your performance is compared with a large, carefully chosen reference sample of people your age. The scale is set so that the average is 100 and the standard deviation is 15. A score of 115 therefore means you scored one standard deviation above the average of that reference group.

What the tests actually measure

Professional tests combine several kinds of tasks: verbal reasoning and vocabulary, working memory (holding and manipulating information), processing speed, and non-verbal or "fluid" reasoning such as completing visual patterns. Scores on these different tasks tend to correlate, which is why a single summary score is useful, but most professional reports also describe strengths and weaknesses across areas.

Professional tests versus online tests

A clinical IQ assessment is administered one-to-one by a trained psychologist, follows strict standardised instructions, and is scored against norms collected from a representative sample of the population. A free online test, including this one, cannot do any of that: you choose when and where to take it, the people who take it are a self-selected group, and there is no examiner. Online tests are useful for practice and curiosity, but they are not a substitute for a professional assessment and should never be used for diagnosis or important decisions.
