Are online IQ tests accurate? What to trust and what to ignore

The real limitations of online IQ tests: norms, self-selection, practice effects and conditions, and how to read any online result sensibly.

The norm problem

An IQ score only means something relative to a reference group. Professional tests are normed on large samples chosen to represent the population by age, education and region. Most online tests have no such sample. Some invent their norms, and many quietly inflate scores to make results more shareable. Be wary of any free test that tells almost everyone they are well above average.

Self-selection

Even when an online test builds norms from its own users, as this one does, those users are not a random sample of the population. People who seek out reasoning tests may differ from people who don't. That is why this site describes its index as relative to "people who took this test", not to the general population.

Practice effects

Scores improve when people become familiar with a type of question. This is well documented for professional tests too, which is why they are not supposed to be repeated within short periods. If you retake online tests often, expect your scores to drift upward without any real change in ability.

Conditions matter

Distractions, fatigue, a small phone screen, time pressure and testing in a second language all affect performance. A professional assessment controls these conditions; at home, you have to control them yourself.

How to read an online result

Treat it as a rough, friendly indicator of how you do on this particular set of puzzles today, especially the breakdown by skill, which tells you what to practise. Never use an online score for medical, educational or employment decisions.
