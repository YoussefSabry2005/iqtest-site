How to improve your reasoning test performance (honestly)

What actually helps on reasoning tests (familiarity, strategy, timing and rest) and what the evidence says about brain-training games.

Familiarity helps, and it is fair

Much of the difficulty of an unfamiliar test is simply not knowing the question formats. Working through a few examples of each type (number series, matrices, syllogisms, ordering and rotation) removes that surprise. Our practice pages and strategy guides are built for this.

Use a method, not inspiration

For each question type there is a reliable checklist: for number series, check differences, then ratios, then interleaved sequences; for matrices, analyse shape, number and fill separately; for syllogisms, draw circles and hunt for a counter-example. Methods beat waiting for an idea.

Manage time

Questions usually get harder as you go. Don't spend five minutes on one item; skip it and come back. On this site you can go back and change answers before you finish.

Sleep and conditions

Being rested, choosing a quiet time and using a larger screen for visual puzzles all make a measurable difference to how well you can concentrate.

What about brain-training games?

Large reviews of the research have found that practising a brain-training game reliably makes you better at that game and similar tasks, but there is little evidence that the benefits transfer to broad, everyday intelligence. Practice will help you show what you can do on a test; it is unlikely to change your underlying ability much. Be sceptical of products that promise otherwise.

A one-week plan

Day 1: take the full test to see your skill breakdown. Days 2–6: spend 15 minutes a day on your two weakest skills, reading the guide first and then practising. Day 7: take a fresh test (you will get new questions) and compare the skill breakdown, keeping in mind that some improvement is simply familiarity.
