How scoring works

What the test measures

The test samples reasoning skills that appear in many IQ-style tests: pattern finding, rule induction, deduction and spatial manipulation. It is a free practice tool. It is not a clinical IQ test, has not been professionally standardised, and must not be used for diagnosis, employment or education decisions.

How questions are verified

Questions are generated from explicit rules. Before publication, each one is solved again by a separate program that works only from what you see on screen, using a wider set of rules than the generator. A question is rejected if the solver disagrees with the answer key, if any other option also fits a reasonable rule, if options are duplicated, or if the explanation is missing. Verbal questions written with AI must also be solved "blind" by a second, independent AI check with high confidence and no other defensible answer.

How your score is calculated

Each question has a difficulty on a Rasch (item response theory) scale. Your answers produce an ability estimate with an uncertainty range. Because every question's difficulty is known, the score accounts for how hard your particular set of questions was: 12 correct on a harder set scores higher than 12 correct on an easier one. Difficulties are re-estimated from real anonymous answers as more people take the test.

The Reasoning Index uses an IQ-style scale (average 100, standard deviation 15) relative to people who have taken this test. That group is self-selected, not a representative population sample. Until 500 tests have been completed, scores are marked provisional and percentiles are not shown.

Current status: provisional (2 of 500 completed tests collected). Question bank: 382 verified questions (Number patterns 55, Letter patterns 55, Number relationships 55, Matrix reasoning 55, Spatial rotation 54, Logical deduction 54, Ordering & relations 54).

Limits

  • A 20-question test has a margin of error; that's why we show a range.
  • Practice, fatigue, language and screen size all affect scores.
  • Online norms reflect the people who chose to take the test.
